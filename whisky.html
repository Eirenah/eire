About the collection

This collection has been built up over a very long time. It was started by my stepfather. When he passed away, part of his collection — and his love of the hobby — came to me, and it is still growing.

Mostly Irish whiskey and Scotch whisky, with a little champagne. Now and then bottles are traded with other collectors, so the shelves keep changing.

How a collection happens 🥃😄

  • It all started with birthdays 🎂: my friends began giving me whisky as a present — one good bottle at a time. 🎁🥃
  • The birthdays kept coming, and so did the bottles… and that’s how I became a whisky collector. 😄🥃🥃
  • Soon the bottles outnumber the special occasions, the shelf turns into a cabinet, and the cabinet starts asking for a friend. 📦😅
  • Every collector knows the rule: the bottle you didn’t buy is the one you keep thinking about. 🤔
  • Best part? Swapping stories — and sometimes bottles — with people who understand why anyone keeps a whiskey unopened for years. 😉 Sláinte! 🍀

A short history of Irish whiskey (and a little Scotch) 📜

  • Uisce beatha. The Irish name means “water of life”; the English word “whiskey” comes from it.
  • 1608. A licence to distil was granted for the Bushmills area of County Antrim — the date Old Bushmills still carries on its labels.
  • 19th century. Irish whiskey was among the most popular whiskies in the world, with large distilleries in Dublin (Jameson’s Bow Street distillery dates from 1780). The column still patented in 1830 by the Irishman Aeneas Coffey was taken up mainly in Scotland for grain whisky.
  • 20th-century decline. Loss of export markets, the trade war with Britain and US Prohibition (1920–1933) closed most Irish distilleries. In 1966 Jameson, Powers and Cork Distilleries merged into Irish Distillers, which moved production to the new Midleton distillery in 1975.
  • Revival. Since the late 20th century, and especially the 2010s, many new distilleries have opened; today Ireland has dozens.
  • Single pot still. A distinctly Irish style, made from a mash of malted and unmalted barley and distilled in copper pot stills — think Redbreast, Green Spot and Powers.
  • Scotch. The earliest written record is in the Exchequer Rolls of 1494 (malt for Friar John Cor “to make aquavitae”). Scotch must be matured in oak casks in Scotland for at least three years; Irish whiskey likewise for at least three years on the island of Ireland. Note the spelling: whiskey in Ireland, whisky in Scotland.
